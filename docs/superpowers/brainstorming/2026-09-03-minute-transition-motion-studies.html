UTC Clock Motion Studies

Five ways the widget could behave when the minute changes, running side by side on the same clock. Each mock is laid out at the widget's real size and colors: 194 × 48 px, Cascadia Mono 34 px digits in 21 px cells on #18181B, and moves in whole pixels the way GDI does. Only the digits that change ever move. The blue-bordered study is the recommendation.

What I would build

The damped carry roll. Each digit that changes rolls upward through an invisible 28 px window that exactly brackets the digit's ink: the old digit leaves through the top while the new one rises in from below and seats on the baseline. Both dim toward the surface color as they approach the edge of the window, so the cut is softest where the most of a glyph is missing. The roll starts from rest and stops into a detent, with zero velocity at both ends, and lasts 280 ms per digit. When two or more digits change, the digit to the left starts 40 ms after its neighbor, the way a mechanical counter carries. The colon, the unchanged digits and the UTC label never move, and at rest every frame is pixel-identical to today's rendering.

Why this one over the quieter options: you asked to animate the change of the numbers, and the roll is the only transition whose intermediate frames still describe that change truthfully. A half-9 above a half-0 reads as “9 becoming 0”, and the upward direction says “counting up”, which a fade cannot say. Three independent judges and the pure-GDI feasibility probe all arrived at this geometry; the dimming and the zero-velocity start were grafted in from the runners-up to fix the two things that make a bare odometer look crude at 34 px, a full-contrast horizontal cut and a first frame that kicks.

It is also the one design that degrades to every alternative by changing constants. Set the dimming to zero and it is the strict odometer. Shorten travel to 10 px and reshape the fade and it is the slide-fade. Set travel to zero and it is the dip. If the roll turns out to pull your eye after a week in the taskbar, the fallback is a number, not a redesign.

The motion, specified

DirectionAlways upward, in place. The outgoing glyph exits through the top of the window, the incoming one enters from below. Never reverses, including 9→0 and 23:59→00:00.
Duration280 ms per digit. Visible motion occupies about 200 ms of that (frames 2–15 at 15.6 ms), with a ~31 ms still lead-in and a ~46 ms seated tail.
Easinge(t) = 1 − (1 − t)³ (1 + 3t). Zero velocity at both ends, peak speed at t = 1/3 (about 2.8 px per frame), e(0.5) = 0.6875. Integer offsets per tick: 0, 0, 2, 4, 6, 9, 11, 14, 17, 19, 22, 23, 25, 26, 27, 28.
Travel28 px, equal to the window height. Digit ink is rows 14–37 of the 48 px widget; the window is rows 12–40. Because ink (24 rows) is shorter than travel, a constant 4-row gap separates the two half-glyphs, so they never fuse.
DimmingText color = lerp(#FAFAFA → #18181B, 0.7 × displacement / 28). A glyph at rest is full white; a glyph at the window edge is 30% ink. Tunable 0.6–0.8; an exponent on the curve can front-load the dim if the early cut is too bright.
Carry lag40 ms per digit, right to left. Total 280 / 320 / 360 / 400 ms for 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 digits. Visible as a wave when watched directly, not from the corner of the eye. Tunable: 0 or 40.
ColonStatic anchor. Never moves, dims or blinks.
Hour and midnightTreated as a carry, not a reset. 12:59→13:00 rolls three digits; 09:59→10:00 and 23:59→00:00 roll all four, everything seated by 400 ms.
Anything elseAny change that is not exactly one minute forward snaps with no motion: first paint, resume from sleep, a clock correction, or a new minute arriving while a roll is in flight.
Reduced motionFollows Windows “Animation effects” by default, re-read when the setting changes, with a persisted right-click override. Off means off: a single repaint, exactly today's behavior but now landing on the second boundary. Remote Desktop sessions also snap.

One thing you should know before choosing. Windows currently reports client-area animations disabled on this machine (the probes read the live setting directly). The persisted preference in the registry says on, so something in this session switched it off at runtime rather than you flipping the Accessibility toggle. Two consequences: if the widget follows Windows, it would not animate here until you right-click and tick “Animate minute change”, and the widget should re-read the flag at every minute boundary instead of caching it, because a runtime setter may broadcast no change notice. The menu label would show which source is in charge, for example “Animate minute change (following Windows: off)”, so one right-click explains it and one click fixes it.

The alternatives on the board

  • Strict odometer. Same geometry and curve, no dimming. The purest instrument reading and the loudest option: full-contrast half-glyphs with a hard horizontal cut on every moving frame, and any timer hitch shows as a doubled step on a bright glyph. Try it by setting one constant.
  • Short slide-fade. The iOS and Fluent numeric transition: 10 px of travel, 250 ms, the old digit gone in about three frames, the new one brightening as it seats. Calmest of the moving options and the best mid-transition legibility. Most viewers will describe it as a fade with a nudge rather than a roll, and its last 100 ms is 1 px hops on a near-white glyph, the regime where integer-pixel GDI looks jittery.
  • Dip. No motion: the old digit fades into the surface, the new one fades out of it. Quietest and simplest. Direction carries no information, the cell is unreadable for a third of the transition, and at 09:59, 19:59 and 23:59 the whole clock briefly shows only the colon and the label, which peripheral vision reads as a flicker as readily as a roll.

Set aside, and why

  • Split flap. Measured: squashing a 12-row half-glyph with StretchBlt either drops rows (the surviving rows change every frame and shimmer) or, with HALFTONE, averages the counter of an 8 into a flat bar. A believable flap also relies on shading, which your rules forbid.
  • Scale or pop. A new font size per frame re-hints the outlines, so stem weights flicker rather than grow, and a tasteful 5% pop is about 1 px at this size.
  • Wipe or reveal. Directionless: it says something changed, not that time advanced.
  • Horizontal slide. Wrong metaphor for a counter.

How it would be built

Pure GDI, no new dependencies, Native AOT unchanged. Roughly 250–350 lines across four files.

  • Back buffer. One 194 × 48 screen-compatible bitmap in a memory DC, created once from the window DC before the window is shown, with the first frame already rendered into it. Every frame is composed there and WM_PAINT becomes a single BitBlt, so a compositor pass can never catch a half-drawn frame. Torn down DC first, then bitmap.
  • Per-digit drawing. The five characters are drawn individually with ExtTextOut and a clip rectangle. Measured on this machine: 21 px cells starting at x = 15, 36, 57, 78, 99, cell top y = 1, baseline 38. Drawn this way the resting widget differs from today's single DrawText by zero pixels, checked for sixteen different times. GDI applies no kerning to simple Latin text, so whole-string and per-cell layout use the same integer advances.
  • Frame pacing. The probes measured SetTimer at 15.64 ms against a 16.67 ms display refresh, a beat that drops about one compose frame per roll. So frames are paced by DwmFlush (one flat import) with progress computed from Stopwatch elapsed time, never a frame count. SetTimer at 10 ms is the fallback if composition is unavailable; 16 ms is never used because it measured bimodal at 15.6 and 31.2 ms.
  • Minute alignment. Today's 1-second timer has arbitrary phase, so the change can land up to a second late; once the change is visible that reads as a slow clock. The tick re-arms itself to the wall-clock second, and the minute change is detected by comparing strings, never by assuming the tick landed on zero. The taskbar re-assert keeps running every tick.
  • Pure logic, tested. Cell layout, the easing curve, the color lerp, the changed-digit mask, the one-minute guard and the frame schedule live in two new Services files with no Win32 dependency, so the existing test runner covers them, including a test that pins the exact integer offsets above.
  • Setting. The position file gains a nullable “animate” flag. Save must preserve it, and “--reset” leaves it alone.

Why not GDI+ or Direct2D: both were spiked under Native AOT and work, but GDI+ lays out the same font 2 px higher with 20 px instead of 21 px advances, so the resting digits would have to move too and the resting render would visibly change. Direct2D adds about 0.8 MB to a 2.7 MB executable, a resident D3D device and some 300 lines of hand-written COM vtables for a 194 × 48 surface that animates 300 ms a minute. Nothing the roll needs is exclusive to either.

What the adversarial check changed

Three verifiers were asked to refute the design. The geometry, the zero-pixel resting diff, the 4-row gap and the ClearType behavior all held up when reproduced. These claims did not, and the design above already reflects the corrections:

  • The clipped edge is not “never a hard cut”. Brightness tracks displacement, so a glyph missing 2 rows is still at 90% ink. The cut is softest in the middle third of the roll and near full contrast at both ends. It reads as an odometer in motion, but the promise was false as worded.
  • SetTimer pacing would show a small snag most minutes because of the 64 Hz versus 60 Hz beat; DwmFlush pacing replaced it.
  • The synthesis wanted to skip the taskbar re-assert during a roll. That would only lengthen the time an auto-hide taskbar covers the widget, so it stays on every tick.
  • Visible motion is about 200 ms of the 280 ms budget, not 240.
  • The 40 ms carry lag is a cue for someone watching, not a “wave” peripheral vision will notice; it is kept as a tunable rather than a differentiator.
  • “Off means off” under reduced motion is right, but for the reason that Windows removes its own fades when that switch is off, not for photosensitivity.
  • The animations-off reading is live but not persisted: the registry preference says on. So it is not attributed to you, and the widget re-reads the flag at each minute boundary rather than caching it at startup.
  • Small implementation traps caught early: the color lerp needs a pinned rounding rule before its unit-test oracle means anything; the back buffer must exist and hold a frame before the first WM_PAINT, since paint becomes a pure blit; and the memory DC must be deleted before its bitmap or the bitmap leaks.
  • Given that this machine currently reports animation effects off, the shipped defaults should sit at the quiet end (dimming 0.7–0.8, never the strict odometer by default), and a one-week check should be an explicit acceptance test: if minute changes pull your eye while working, step down in this order: dimming 0.8, then carry lag 0, then duration 220 ms, then the slide-fade constants.

Two decisions that are yours

Default behavior. Follow the Windows “Animation effects” switch (so on this machine, as it currently reports, it starts off, with the right-click item to turn it on), or animate by default regardless of that setting?

Carry lag. Keep the 40 ms right-to-left lag so a carry is visible as a cascade when watched (an hour change takes 360–400 ms), or roll all changing digits together so every change is exactly 280 ms?

Everything else in the table is a tunable I can settle by watching one carry and one hour change on the real widget once it is built.
